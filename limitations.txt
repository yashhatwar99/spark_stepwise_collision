LIMITATIONS OF EACH PAPER (plain language)
=============================================
Based on reading each paper's own text (conclusion, limitations, and
results sections) -- not guessed. Papers are the 14 from the earlier
GPU/model-size list, sorted the same way.


1. ROAR (2025)
---------------
- Still makes mistakes: sometimes warns when there's no danger (false
  alarm), sometimes misses a real accident (false negative).
- Struggles most with small or far-away accidents -- hard to notice in
  the video.
- Hasn't actually been tested for live, real-time use yet -- the authors
  list "real-time application" as future work, meaning it isn't proven
  yet.
- Built specifically to fix sensor noise and uneven data problems, which
  means those were real weak points in accident-prediction models before
  it, and testing on more traffic conditions is still needed.


2. Predict and Resist (2025)
------------------------------
- Needs noisy and clean versions of the same scene to learn how to clean
  up a noisy video -- depends on having that kind of training data.
- More moving parts than most models (a noise-cleaning step plus a
  decision-making step), which likely makes it slower and heavier to run.
- Built to fix two specific problems (sensor noise, and deciding *when*
  to warn) -- doesn't claim to fix other issues like unusual road layouts
  or rare situations.


3. RARE / Feature Reuse (2025)
---------------------------------
- Its speed comes from reusing another detector's leftover features --
  so if that detector misses a vehicle, RARE has nothing to work with for
  that vehicle.
- Does not use optical flow (motion-tracking between frames) at all,
  unlike most other models -- it may miss some motion-based warning signs
  that heavier models would catch.
- Depends entirely on one single pretrained object detector; there's no
  backup if that detector performs poorly on a new kind of video.


4. Real-time Collision Risk from Monocular Video (2019)
-----------------------------------------------------------
- Assumes each car keeps a constant speed and direction when predicting
  risk -- doesn't handle sudden braking or swerving well.
- Needs several hand-set numbers (camera height, assumed car width,
  camera tilt) that must be re-measured for a new camera or vehicle.
- Fairly slow: about 5.5 FPS with the simple risk model, dropping to
  about 3.4-4.9 FPS with the more advanced version -- not fast enough for
  full real-world driving speed.
- The authors admit they have no ground-truth data to properly check how
  accurate their own object tracker is -- they list this as future work.
- Assumes the distance/speed readings have very little noise, with no
  real smoothing or filtering applied.


5. Anticipating Accidents in Dashcam Videos / DAD dataset (2016)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- One of the earliest papers in this area -- its accuracy (about 48% AP)
  is far below newer methods (often 70-90%+), so it's now mainly
  historical.
- Its own DAD dataset is a known weak point: later papers found it has
  very few real "ego-vehicle involved" accidents (around 92% of its
  labeled accidents don't actually involve the camera's own car).
- Built on older building blocks (VGG + LSTM) that have since been
  replaced by lighter and more accurate networks.


6. UString / CCD dataset (2020)
-----------------------------------
- Accuracy drops noticeably when it's asked to catch almost every real
  accident (high recall) -- more real accidents caught means more false
  alarms too.
- Relies on a separate object detector (Faster/Cascade R-CNN) running
  first to find vehicles -- if that detector misses or mislabels a
  vehicle, the rest of the pipeline is affected.
- Uses a Bayesian neural network to estimate uncertainty, which needs
  multiple passes through the network at test time (10 passes) -- more
  compute than a plain network.


7. DSTA (2021)
------------------
- Performs very differently on different datasets (DAD vs CCD), and the
  authors say openly that they don't know the exact reason why -- its
  behavior on a brand-new dataset is hard to predict.
- Needs a large, 32GB datacenter GPU (Tesla V100) for training, hinting
  this is not a lightweight model.
- Combines multiple trained networks together (late-fusion), which adds
  extra computation at prediction time.


8. STAGNet (2025)
----------------------
- The full model is not optimized for speed -- the authors had to build
  a separate faster "Lite" version, which trades away a bit of accuracy
  for speed.
- Accuracy drops noticeably when tested on a dataset it wasn't trained
  on, and the authors say the exact reason for this drop is unclear.
- Uses simple rectangular boxes around vehicles, which can include empty
  background space -- less precise than outlining a vehicle's real shape.
- Doesn't use real-world distance between vehicles at all -- the authors
  suggest adding this later to improve results.


9. VAGNet (2026)
---------------------
- Depends heavily on using a large, powerful video-understanding model
  (VideoMAE-V2) to reach its best accuracy -- swapping it for an older,
  lighter model (VGG16) drops accuracy a lot (e.g. 77.98% down to 54.83%
  AP on one dataset).
- Still relies on existing dashcam datasets for training, which the
  authors say don't cover every real driving situation -- more diverse
  data is listed as future work.
- Its reported speed (38 FPS) was measured on the authors' own hardware;
  other methods report higher raw speed, but on more powerful GPUs.


10. Domain-Enhanced Dual-Branch Model (2025)
-------------------------------------------------
- Fails in poor visual conditions -- the authors show a real example
  where it misses an accident because of bad lighting and a far-away
  distance between vehicles.
- Uses a Large Language Model (GPT-4o) as part of the system, which is
  heavy and depends on an external AI service working correctly.
- Collecting and storing accident reports raises data-privacy concerns,
  requiring extra protection (encryption, anonymizing personal info).
- Since it saves its own predictions to learn from later, a wrong
  prediction (false alarm) can end up polluting its own future training
  data unless it's manually checked.


11. FLaRA (2026)
---------------------
- Needs a minimum 2-second (16-frame) video clip before it can make any
  prediction at all -- it can't warn earlier than that, which hurts
  results on datasets where accidents happen very quickly (like DAD,
  where accidents start after only ~3 seconds on average).
- Built on top of a large pretrained video model (V-JEPA2); even though
  only part of it is fine-tuned, the full model still has 84 million
  parameters to run.
- Gets noticeably less accurate the further in advance it's asked to
  predict -- easier close to the event, harder further ahead.


12. RiskProp (2026)
------------------------
- Still needs the exact collision frame correctly labeled during
  training -- it removes the need for "accident start" labels, but not
  for knowing exactly when the collision itself happens.
- Only tested on two datasets (CAP and Nexar) -- not yet shown to work
  equally well on other kinds of dashcam footage.
- Assumes danger should generally keep rising the closer you get to a
  collision -- in real driving, a near-miss can spike briefly and then
  drop again, which this assumption doesn't fully capture.


13. BADAS (2025)
---------------------
- Much worse at recognizing rare situations -- pedestrians, cyclists,
  and especially animals get noticeably lower recall than regular
  vehicle-to-vehicle cases, because there's less training data for them.
- Its best version (BADAS1.0) needs a large private dataset (40,000
  videos) that isn't publicly available; the public version (BADAS-Open)
  is trained on far less data (1,500 videos) and performs worse.
- The authors note their model (like others tested) sometimes predicts
  "too early" compared to real human judgment -- in a real car, that
  would show up as false alarms.


14. Nexar Dashcam Collision Prediction Dataset and Challenge (2025)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
- This is mainly a dataset-and-competition paper, not a single trained
  model -- so its limitations are about the data, not an algorithm.
- Collected from specific US urban/suburban areas -- may not represent
  driving conditions elsewhere.
- Doesn't yet deeply cover extreme weather, multi-vehicle pile-ups, or
  rare road-user types -- the authors list expanding to these as future
  work.


NOTE ON COVERAGE
=================
Two papers in the earlier list had almost no text I could match to
"limitation" or "future work" keywords (Chan et al. 2016 and UString
2020) -- for those, the points above come from their results tables and
conclusion sections instead, plus what later papers (DSTA, VAGNet) say
about their weaknesses. Everything above is grounded in the papers'
actual text, not invented.
